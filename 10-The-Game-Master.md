## The Role of the Game Master

The GM creates, maintains, and describes the world in which the player characters (PCs) live.
He sets the scene, guides the plot, and decides the outcome of the players' actions. In many
ways he is like a film director — but unlike a film, a roleplaying game is a collaborative
effort, and it ultimately falls to the players to decide what their characters do. In addition
to being director and storyteller, the GM is also referee: he applies the rules fairly and
impartially and makes sure everyone has a good time. He also plays every NPC in the game.

**The basics — three things a GM should consider:**
- **Know the rules.** The GM should know the rules at least as well as the other players and
  should read as much of the book as possible before running his first game. Chapter IX:
  Playing the Game is particularly important, since it covers the game's core mechanics.
- **Know the plot.** The GM does not need to know every detail of a scenario in advance, but he
  should know at least where it begins and where it is heading. A plot can be as simple as
  delivering a rare cargo past a renegade Imperial captain, or as complex as undermining the
  authority of several planetary governors to destabilise an entire region. Any preparation is
  rarely wasted, and pre-written adventures (such as the one in Chapter XV: Into the Maw) can
  also be used.
- **Choosing the GM.** Ideally a volunteer; often the person who organised the game or owns the
  book. Several players may take turns running the game over time, sharing the workload and
  contributing their own adventures.

**Delegating to players:** players can help with logistics (dice, snacks, and so on) and add
depth to the setting — for example, writing up the background of their own character's home
world, which both eases the GM's workload and enriches the group's adventures. Being prepared
also means making sure the group has everything it needs to play (dice, paper, miniatures);
some GMs like to keep players' character sheets between sessions so they don't go missing.

## The Dark Frontier — Evoking the Setting

Rogue Trader is a game of adventure, exploration, and glory set against the dark backdrop of
the 41st millennium. As the crew of a Rogue Trader's starship, the players are uniquely
powerful individuals with a destiny and power far beyond that of ordinary citizens. Part of the
GM's job is to bring this universe to life by weaving in its key themes:

- **A License for Profit:** Rogue Traders exist for a single purpose — the accumulation of
  wealth and power — under a warrant granted by the Administratum of Holy Terra, which lets
  them travel and trade both within and beyond the Imperium's borders.
- **The Ancient and the Forgotten:** the Imperium is an ancient empire steeped in mysticism,
  fear, and superstition. Even a Rogue Trader relies on the barely-understood, stagnant
  technology of the Adeptus Mechanicus, and few charts exist even of the Imperium's own worlds.
- **Into the Unknown:** Rogue Traders are prolific explorers by necessity — a unique breed
  within a society built on the censure of knowledge and the restriction of travel.
- **Fear the Void:** beyond the deadly cold of vacuum and the hard radiation of stars lie things
  no man was meant to see — precisely what makes Rogue Traders larger-than-life figures who set
  off into the dark with often only their courage to protect them.

### What if no one wants to be the Rogue Trader?

The crew's Rogue Trader could be incapacitated but still hold the title (encased in a stasis
field, bedridden, barely conscious) while another player interprets his wishes and commands in
his stead; or he might not be aboard the players' ship at all, commanding his fleet from a
conquered world or a seldom-seen command ship. Either way, the GM should use the Rogue Trader's
influence sparingly and leave most decisions in the players' own hands.

### What if more than one player wants to be the Rogue Trader?

Options include: (1) the Rogue Traders are siblings, cousins, or otherwise part of the same
dynasty — an NPC patriarch may hold the actual Warrant of Trade, or one player may hold it while
the others are assistants and heirs apparent; (2) the group commands multiple ships, each with
its own Warrant, as a small allied fleet — perhaps bound by an arranged marriage between
families; (3) the Rogue Traders pool their resources to jointly purchase a single vessel and are
all "shareholders" in the venture — for instance, younger sons and daughters of established
lines with no prospects of their own, or survivors of some failed venture, war, or crusade.

## How to Run a Rogue Trader Adventure

The PCs are the crew of a starship with the means and licence to travel between Imperial worlds
and beyond, so finding reasons for adventure is never hard. The focus of most adventures is
profit and the acquisition of wealth, often combined with exploration, conflict, and combat.

### Styles of play (freely combinable)

- **Exploring the Calixis Sector:** even within the settled sector there is ample uncharted
  void — the Adranti Nebula, the Hazeroth Abyss, and the mysterious Tyrant Star (whose passage
  leaves dead worlds worth investigating) all offer adventure locales.
- **Trade:** the core of most adventures — carrying cargo from place to place and selling it at
  a profit. Hard-to-reach destinations command higher prices. Smuggling is a natural companion
  to trade and a good springboard into darker plots. Trade gives an easy, universal
  motivation: simple greed.
- **Exploration:** the Imperium is vast and largely uncharted; lost worlds, untapped resources,
  and new warp routes can bring riches and glory. Rogue Traders are sometimes even employed by
  Adeptus Mechanicus Explorator fleets for their knowledge.
- **The Great Game:** rising to sector-spanning power through sponsorship, guild membership, and
  the favour of Imperial officials, eventually amassing wealth, mercenaries, ships, and even
  whole worlds. This requires genuinely invested players and more GM work, but can produce truly
  memorable campaigns.
- **Conquest and Piracy:** risky and expensive, and rarely a true path to wealth on its own, but
  exciting and easily combined with the other styles — after all, what is a Rogue Trader's life
  without a little danger?

### Rogue Trader dynasties

A Rogue Trader and his crew are more than a captain and his crew — they are part of a grand
legacy often stretching back centuries. All the PCs, not just the Rogue Trader player, are part
of this dynasty and are recognised as rare, chosen individuals with the power to shape the very
destiny of the Imperium. The GM should therefore spread the focus of his adventures and plots
across all the characters rather than centring everything on the Rogue Trader alone.

### The role of the crew

Every character fills a vital role aboard ship. When planning adventures, the GM should
consider each PC's Skills and try to make use of them during play, giving every player a chance
to shine. Imperial ships often carry many thousands (even tens of thousands) of crew; the GM
generally does not need names or descriptions for these NPCs, though important recurring crew
members can be given extra depth.

## Rewards: The Measure of Success

Rogue Traders take terrible risks — and are rewarded for it. Rewards come as experience points
(which improve a character's Skills and abilities), Profit rewards (which add to the crew's
wealth), and game rewards (rank, privilege, and title).

### Experience Points (XP)

Two main methods for awarding XP:

**Abstract Method** (easiest, recommended for beginning GMs): each player receives **500 XP**
per game session, assuming a session lasts about 4 hours; adjust proportionally for longer or
shorter sessions.

**Detailed Method:** the GM assigns each encounter a value based on its difficulty and awards XP
when the encounter is completed or overcome, per **Table 10-1: Encounter Difficulty**:

| Difficulty | XP Award |
|---|---|
| Easy | 50 |
| Routine | 70 |
| Ordinary | 100 |
| Average | 130 |
| Challenging | 170 |
| Hard | 200 |
| Very Hard | 250 |

To judge an encounter's difficulty, consider how much it taxes the group's resources and what
it costs them in Wounds, equipment, or time. Award experience only for meaningful encounters and
obstacles — not for the wholesale slaughter of worlds simply for XP — and remember that as PCs
grow more powerful, some challenges will be worth little or nothing.

**Roleplaying Awards:** the GM may also grant 100–500 XP for particularly good roleplaying, such
as a player taking an action that endangers his character simply because "it's what his
character would do." Use sparingly, and only when genuinely deserved.

### Profit Factor Rewards

Most Profit Factor gains come from Endeavours (see Chapter IX), since amassing wealth or
influence takes time and planning. The GM may nonetheless award **+1 Profit Factor** for
outstanding actions or plot outcomes — sparingly, and never by more than 1 point at a time.

### Game Rewards

Influence, power, and rank earned by completing quests for powerful NPCs or through actions that
alter the fabric of the game world. These are harder to adjudicate but can be more valuable than
any other kind of reward, granting things that skill with a bolter or a bigger ship never could.

### Awarding Fate Points

The GM may occasionally reward a truly remarkable deed with a permanent additional Fate Point —
use this very sparingly, and only for something incredible.

### When to use rewards

Most rewards are given at the end of a session (or at the start of the next, giving the GM time
to tally achievements). Profit and game rewards, however, may be handed out during play as they
are earned, since they fluctuate constantly over the course of a Rogue Trader's adventures.

## Mass Combats

Rogue Trader can involve pitched battles where the fate of worlds hangs in the balance. There
are two ways to handle mass combat: the Detailed Method and the Simple Method.

### The Detailed Method

The GM groups similar combatants on each side into **units** of between 3 and 20 combatants. A
unit acts like a single individual, armed with the most common weapon and armour of its members,
with Wounds equal to its number of members plus a damage bonus of +1 for every 4 members. For
example, a company of 40 guardsmen with lasguns, 6 heavy bolter weapon teams, and 20 veteran
shotgunners would form three units: 20 lasgun-armed guardsmen (20 Wounds, +5 damage bonus), a
heavy-bolter team (12 Wounds — i.e. 6 members — +3 damage bonus), and 20 veterans with shotguns
(20 Wounds, +5 damage bonus).

Special rules:
- Units always act after individuals (PCs, powerful creatures) in the Initiative order.
- All units act simultaneously in Initiative, unless a unit has surprise or another special
  advantage.
- A unit reduced to 0 Wounds can no longer fight (though the GM may rule some members survive).
- Individuals cannot fight units directly and vice versa — single members of a unit can attack
  individuals, and individuals can single out members of a unit as targets.
- Units make all other Tests (e.g. Fear Tests) as a single entity; social/influence Skills can
  likewise be used against a unit as if it were an individual.

### The Simple Method

Each PC makes a single Skill Test suited to his role in the battle — a Weapon Skill Test for a
PC in the thick of melee, a Command Test for one barking orders over the vox, and so on.
Difficulty is set by the GM based on enemy strength, terrain, or anything else relevant. The GM
sets a target number of successes (e.g. 10 for victory, 5 for a lesser victory, 0 for a draw).
Failure might inflict damage (e.g. 1d5 per Degree of Failure) — such arbitrary damage should
never kill a character, only leave them severely wounded or incapacitated. The GM can optionally
make one additional combined Skill roll to represent the effect of the PCs' allies.

## Interaction

Much of Rogue Trader centres on dealing with the many peoples of the Imperium. Chapter III:
Skills presents the Interaction Skills — Charm, Command, Deceive, and Intimidate — basic
Fellowship Skills usable by any character, albeit at reduced effectiveness without training.
Charm is used to befriend, Command to issue orders, Deceive to fool or dupe, and Intimidate to
frighten.

Usually a player doesn't need to Test to interact with NPCs — he simply roleplays his character,
and the outcome follows from the conversation between player and GM. A Test becomes necessary
when failure would carry consequences, or when a player is trying to make an NPC do something
they would not ordinarily do. Ultimately, when a Test is required is up to the GM.

### Dispositions

The base Difficulty of an Interaction Test is set by the GM according to the NPC's
**disposition** toward the character. **Table 10-2: Dispositions** gives sample dispositions by
Skill and Difficulty:

| Difficulty | Modifier | Charm | Command | Deceive | Intimidate |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| Easy | +30 | Infatuated | Fanatical | Gullible | Terrified |
| Routine | +20 | Affectionate | Devoted | Trusting | Frightened |
| Ordinary | +10 | Favourable | Loyal | Accepting | Startled |
| Challenging | +0 | Indifferent | Indifferent | Indifferent | Indifferent |
| Difficult | -10 | Disdainful | Resentful | Suspicious | Brave |
| Hard | -20 | Contemptful | Disloyal | Sceptical | Courageous |
| Very Hard | -30 | Disgusted | Mutinous | Disbelieving | Foolhardy |

On a successful Test, the target's disposition improves by one step, plus one further step per
Degree of Success. On a failed Test, disposition worsens by one step per Degree of Failure. A
target must be at least Indifferent to comply at all, and even then will only do as asked 50% of
the time left to his own devices; each step above Indifferent adds 10% to that chance, and being
under direct supervision adds a further 20%.

### Interaction and groups

Against a group, the GM sets a disposition based on the group's overall attitude. On a
successful Test, the player affects a number of individuals equal to his Fellowship Bonus
(certain Talents can increase this number).

## Fear and Damnation

Three interlinking areas: **Fear** (immediate reaction to frightening events), **Insanity**
(long-term mental effects), and **Corruption** (the insidious influence of the warp and
forbidden knowledge). During combat, only the Fear rules apply — Insanity and Corruption are
resolved afterwards.

### Fear Tests

When confronted by something frightening, a character makes a **Willpower Test**, modified by
severity per **Table 10-3: Fear Test Difficulties**:

| Severity of Fear | Test Mod | Example Adversaries |
|---|---|---|
| Fear (1) Disturbing | 0 | Rak'Gol Marauder |
| Fear (2) Frightening | -10 | Ebon Geist |
| Fear (3) Horrifying | -20 | Yu'vath Bone Warden |
| Fear (4) Terrifying | -30 | The Walking Nightmare |

Fear Tests in non-combat situations are entirely the GM's call and should not be automatic —
use them sparingly to build dramatic tension. Example triggers: *Disturbing* — the scene of a
gruesome murder, minor unexplained supernatural phenomena (strange lights, spectral voices,
weeping statues); *Frightening* — meeting someone known to be dead, being buried alive, major
supernatural phenomena (skies raining blood, a painting forming shrieking mouths); *Horrifying*
— a sudden, inexplicable death nearby, waking covered in blood surrounded by bodies with no
memory of how you got there, the dead rising across a whole planet; *Terrifying* — a full-scale
daemonic incursion, discovering your own failure caused millions of deaths, staring unprotected
into the warp.

If the Test succeeds, the character acts normally. If it fails during combat, immediately roll
on **Table 10-4: The Shock Table** (`1d100`, +10 per Degree of Failure):

| Roll | Result |
|---|---|
| 01–20 | Badly startled: only a single Half Action next Turn, then acts normally. |
| 21–40 | Shakes and trembles: -10 to all Tests for the rest of the encounter unless he snaps out of it. |
| 41–60 | Reels with shock, backs away, cannot willingly approach the object of his fear; -10 to all Tests until the end of the encounter; gains 1 Insanity Point. |
| 61–80 | Frozen by terror, no Actions until he snaps out of it; afterwards -10 to all Tests for the rest of the encounter; gains 1d5 Insanity Points. |
| 81–100 | Panics: must flee if able, or is limited to Half Actions at -20 to all Tests; must snap out of it once away from danger; gains 1d5 Insanity Points. |
| 101–120 | Faints, unconscious for 1d5 Rounds; afterwards -10 to all Tests until the end of the encounter; gains 1d5 Insanity Points. |
| 121–130 | Screams and vomits uncontrollably for 1d5 Rounds (helpless, drops held items); afterwards only a single Half Action per Turn until able to rest; gains 1d5 Insanity Points. |
| 131–140 | Laughs hysterically and randomly attacks anything nearby until he snaps out of it or is knocked unconscious; gains 1d5 Insanity Points. |
| 141–160 | Crumples to the ground for 1d5+1 Rounds, sobbing and tearing at his own flesh; afterwards -20 to all Tests until the end of the encounter; gains 1d5+1 Insanity Points. |
| 161–170 | Mind snaps: catatonic for 1d5 hours, may not be roused; gains 1d10 Insanity Points. |
| 171+ | Suffers "acute hallucinations" (see Disorders) for 2d10 Rounds; afterwards -20 to all Tests for the rest of the encounter; gains 2d10 Insanity Points and 1d10 points of permanent Willpower damage. |

**Outside combat:** on a failed Fear Test, the character suffers -10 to any Skill or Test
requiring concentration for as long as he remains near the object of his fear (simply leaving
and returning does not remove this). If the Test is failed by 30 or more, he also gains +1d5
Insanity Points.

**Snapping out of it:** where the Shock Table specifies it, a character may attempt a Willpower
Test at the start of his next Turn to shrug off the effect; he may keep retesting at the start of
each subsequent Turn until he succeeds.

## Insanity Points and Corruption Points

Both are Characteristics that begin at 0 and increase over time. If either ever reaches 100, the
character is hopelessly driven mad or tainted, and his career — and life — end at once, exactly
as if he had been killed.

### Going Insane

A character's **Degree of Madness** is determined by **Table 10-5: The Insanity Track**:

| Insanity Points | Degree of Madness | Trauma Modifier |
|---|---|---|
| 0–9 | Stable | n/a |
| 10–19 | Unsettled | +10 |
| 20–29 | Unsettled | +10 |
| 30–39 | Unsettled | +10 |
| 40–49 | Disturbed | +0 |
| 50–59 | Disturbed | +0 |
| 60–69 | Unhinged | -10 |
| 70–79 | Unhinged | -10 |
| 80–89 | Deranged | -20 |
| 90–99 | Deranged | -20 |
| 100+ | Terminally Insane | character retires from play |

**Mental Trauma:** each time a character gains 10 Insanity Points, he makes a **Willpower Test**
(modified per the table above). On a failure, roll `1d100` (+10 per Degree of Failure) on
**Table 10-6: Mental Traumas**:

| Roll | Result |
|---|---|
| 01–40 | Withdrawn and quiet: -10 to all Fellowship-based Tests for 3d10 hours. |
| 41–70 | Compulsively performs an action (fevered praying, cleaning a weapon, reciting verse) and pays little attention to anything else; -10 to all Intelligence-, Fellowship-, or Perception-based Tests for 3d10 hours. |
| 71–100 | Constantly fearful and jumpy: +10 to Perception-based Tests, -10 to Willpower, for 1d5 days; gains a temporary Severe Phobia lasting 1d5 days. |
| 101–120 | Extremely agitated: must pass a Willpower Test before any Test or suffer -10 to it; in combat all Tests automatically suffer -10; lasts 1d5 days. |
| 121–130 | Vivid, extreme nightmares whenever sleeping; exhausted by lack of sleep, gains a level of Fatigue the next day and for 1d10 days; lasts 1d5 days. |
| 131–140 | Struck dumb, unable to speak, for 1d5 days. |
| 141–150 | Distressed and unfocused, refuses to eat or drink: -10 to all Characteristics (none may drop below 1) for 1d10 days. |
| 151–160 | Temporarily hysterically blind or deaf for 1d10 days. |
| 161–170 | Completely traumatised and virtually unresponsive — cannot initiate actions but can be gently led — for 1d10 days. |
| 171+ | (see table 161–170 result; extreme cases as above) |

**Gaining Mental Disorders:** a character automatically gains a new disorder (or a worsened
version of an existing one) at 40 IP (**Minor Disorder**), 60 IP (**Severe Disorder**), and 80 IP
(**Acute Disorder**) — corresponding to becoming Disturbed, Unhinged, and Deranged. A disorder
can only worsen if the character already has the preceding severity (except "The Flesh is Weak!"
which has no Minor version). The GM chooses the disorder, or may let the player choose.

**Only the Insane Shall Prosper:** if the first digit of a character's Insanity Points total is
double or more a creature's Fear Rating, the character is unaffected and need not make a Fear
Test against it.

**Disorder severity:** if a disorder would hinder a character in a given situation, he may Test
Willpower to ignore its effects for the encounter. **Minor** disorders grant +10 to this Test,
**Severe** disorders no modifier, and **Acute** disorders a -10 penalty.

**Example disorders:**
- **The Flesh is Weak!** (Severe/Acute only): blames his own flesh for failures, becomes
  obsessed with surgical modification and bionic replacement.
- **Phobia** (Minor/Severe/Acute): must pass a Willpower Test to interact with the object of his
  fear; forced exposure may trigger Fear Tests (examples: Fear of the Dead, Fear of Insects).
- **Obsession/Compulsion** (Minor/Severe/Acute): must pass a Willpower Test to resist compulsive
  behaviour (examples: Kleptomania, Self-Mortification).
- **Visions and Voices** (Minor/Severe/Acute): sees and hears things that aren't there (examples:
  Dead Comrade, Flashbacks).
- **Delusion** (Minor/Severe/Acute): acts on a false belief as though it were true (examples:
  Invulnerability, Righteousness).
- **Horrific Nightmares** (Minor/Severe): must pass a Willpower Test after a stressful day or
  gain a level of Fatigue the next day.

**Removing Insanity Points:** with the GM's permission, a character may spend 100 XP to remove 1
Insanity Point. A character never drops below a Degree of Madness he has already reached, and
never loses disorders already gained. This should be represented in-game (prayer, fasting,
penance, long-term palliative care, recuperation in pleasant surroundings, contemplation of holy
works).

### Corruption

The most insidious threat to humanity is the corrupting taint of Chaos. Encounters with
daemons, dark rituals, and even mere knowledge that such things exist are all paths to
corruption.

**Corruption Points (CP)** work almost exactly like Insanity Points, except they arise from
exposure to the warp, dark rituals, cursed artefacts, and daemonic influence, and lead to
**Malignancies** and **Mutation**. Example sources of CP:

- **Warp Shock:** a failed Fear Test against a warp entity inflicts CP equal to the being's Fear
  Rating (e.g. Frightening (2) = 2 CP).
- **Rending the Veil:** direct intrusion of the warp into reality — anywhere from a single point
  to several d10s of CP depending on severity.
- **Sorcery:** minor rituals typically cause 1d10 CP; major ceremonies cause much more.
- **Blasphemous Lore:** knowledge itself can corrupt — forbidden tomes, pict-logs, debased art.
- **Dark Deeds:** evil acts in furtherance of a Malignancy, forbidden lore, or to appease a
  daemon always cause CP.
- **Vile Persuasion:** corruption sown by the insidious temptation of daemons or cult leaders.

**Table 10-7: The Corruption Track:**

| CP Total | Degree of Corruption | Malignancy Test | Mutation |
|---|---|---|---|
| 01–30 | Tainted | +0 | — |
| 31–60 | Soiled | -10 | First Test |
| 61–90 | Debased | -20 | Second Test |
| 91–99 | Profane | -30 | Third Test |
| 100 | Damned | — | character removed from play |

**Malignancy Test:** for every 10 CP a character gains, he makes a **Willpower Test** (modified
per the table above) to see whether his corruption manifests as literal damage to body and soul.
On a failure, roll `1d100` on **Table 10-8: Malignancies**:

| Roll | Effect |
|---|---|
| 01–10 | **Palsy:** numerous minor tics, shakes, tremors. Reduce Agility by 1d10. |
| 11–15 | **Dark-hearted:** grows cruel, callous, vindictive. Reduce Fellowship by 1d10. |
| 16–20 | **Ill-fortuned:** whenever a Fate Point is used, roll 1d10 — on a 7–10 it has no effect but is lost anyway. |
| 21–22 | **Skin Afflictions:** boils, scabs, weeping sores; -20 to all Charm Tests. |
| 23–25 | **Night Eyes:** light pains the character; -10 to all Tests in bright light unless he shields his eyes. |
| 26–30 | **Morbid:** mind filled with macabre visions; Intelligence reduced by 1d10. |
| 31–33 | **Witch-mark:** a small, concealable physical deformity or mutation — enough to warrant the stake if discovered. |
| 34–45 | **Fell Obsession:** as the Obsession Disorder, but with a sinister or malign focus (finger-bone trophies, ritual scarification, meaningless vivisection). |
| 46–50 | **Hatred:** an implacable hatred of a single group, individual, or social class. |
| 51–55 | **Irrational Nausea:** must Test Toughness on encountering the object of his revulsion or suffer -10 to all Tests while it remains present. |
| 56–60 | **Wasted Frame:** corpse-like pallor, wasting muscles; Strength reduced by 1d10. |
| 61–63 | **Night Terrors:** daemonic visions in sleep — as Horrific Nightmares. |
| 64–70 | **Poor Health:** petty illnesses and phantom pains; Toughness reduced by 1d10. |
| 71–75 | **Distrustful:** -10 to Fellowship Tests when dealing with strangers. |
| 76–80 | **Malign Sight:** the world seems to darken and rot; Perception reduced by 1d10. |
| 81–83 | **Ashen Taste:** food and drink taste disgusting; doubles the negative effects of Fatigue levels. |
| 84–90 | **Bloodlust:** after being wounded in combat, must Test Willpower to allow enemies to flee rather than kill them outright. |
| 91–93 | **Blackouts:** inexplicable blackouts — the GM decides what happens during them. |
| 94–00 | **Strange Addiction:** addicted to something bizarre (drinking blood, the taste of widows' tears); acts as a Minor Compulsion. |

If a player rolls a result he has already suffered, he rolls again.

**Mutation:** for every 30 CP a character gains, he Tests against two Characteristics of his
choice; on failure he suffers a random Minor Mutation (see Chapter XIV: Adversaries & Aliens).
He may never test against the same Characteristic twice to resist mutation, and should note on
his character sheet which Characteristics have already been used.
